For years, I've been saying attention is your most valuable resource. Not as a productivity hack or self-help platitude, but as a fundamental truth about how reality works. When you invest your attention, reality conspires for you. When you scatter it, you lose track of your own life.

Turns out, this isn't just philosophy. It's neuroscience.

Researchers at UNLV just published a study in *Current Biology* that rewrites our understanding of how the brain experiences time. The finding? **Your brain doesn't have an internal clock. It has a counter.**

And what is it counting? *Experiences.* Events. The things you pay attention to.

## The Clock That Isn't There

We've always assumed the brain has some kind of internal timekeeper — a neural pacemaker ticking away in the background, measuring seconds and minutes like a Swiss watch. It made intuitive sense. After all, we can estimate durations, anticipate intervals, and feel the difference between a minute and an hour.

But the UNLV team found something different. Instead of a clock, they discovered relay teams of neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) — a region deep in your brain that's involved in decision-making, error detection, and attention.

Here's how it works: when you engage in an activity, neurons fire in sequence. One group hands off to the next, then the next, like runners passing a baton. The brain doesn't measure *time* — it counts *handoffs*. More handoffs = more perceived time. Fewer handoffs = time compresses.

This is why time flies when you're doing a lot. Not just when you're having fun — when you're *busy*. When your brain is processing a high volume of experiences, the counter racks up quickly. When you're still, waiting, doing nothing? The count barely moves. Time crawls.

## Attention Is the Currency of Experience

If the brain counts experiences rather than measuring time, then the quality and density of your attention directly determines your subjective reality.

Think about it:

- **A meditation retreat** where you spend ten days doing nothing but breathing feels eternal. Your brain is firing on just a few channels. The count is slow. You experience every second.
- **A weekend scrolling Instagram** vanishes in what feels like minutes. Your brain is processing rapid-fire micro-stimuli, but none of it sticks. The count is high, but shallow. You lose the weekend.

This is the core insight of pronoia: *attention is how you construct your experience of reality.* 

You're not a passive observer watching time pass. You're an active participant, and your attention is the tool you use to build the texture, depth, and meaning of your life. When you invest attention deliberately, you get more time. When you let it scatter, time evaporates.

The UNLV study gives us the mechanism. The ACC isn't just tracking what happens *to* you — it's tracking what you *engage with*. Attention isn't peripheral. It's structural. It's how your brain decides what counts as an experience.

## The Hack: Slow Down to Remember, Speed Up to Move On

Here's where this gets practical.

If your brain counts experiences rather than measuring time, you can use this to hack your mental health and memory.

**Want to remember something?** Slow down. Pay deep attention. Let each moment register fully. The more fidelity you bring to an experience, the more your brain counts it as significant. This is why a single mindful meal can feel longer and more satisfying than a week of eating while staring at a screen.

**Want to move past something painful?** Speed up. Fill your attention with activity, novelty, new stimuli. Keep the count moving. This isn't avoidance — it's strategic distraction. When you're processing a breakup or a loss, sitting still and ruminating makes time stretch unbearably. But taking on a new project, traveling, or engaging deeply in work compresses the pain into a smaller subjective window. The calendar days still pass, but your brain counts fewer of them.

This is the difference between *living through* something and *dwelling in* it.

## Happiness Is the Absence of Internal Distance

One of the core ideas of pronoia is that happiness is the absence of internal distance. It's the feeling of alignment with reality — of being fully *here*, not split between where you are and where you wish you were.

The ACC study maps directly onto this.

When you're fully engaged — when your attention is invested rather than divided — the relay teams fire cleanly. Experience flows. You're not watching the clock because your brain isn't trying to escape the present. You're in it.

But when you're distracted, resistant, or elsewhere, the count gets jagged. You're half-here, half-gone. Time feels wrong because your attention is wrong. You're creating internal distance, and your brain registers it as temporal friction.

This is why boredom feels so brutal. It's not the absence of stimulation — it's the presence of resistance. You want to be somewhere else, doing something else, but you're stuck here. The count slows to a crawl because you're refusing to engage. You're withholding attention, and your brain punishes you with stretched, empty time.

The solution isn't more stimulation. It's less resistance. **Invest your attention in what's actually in front of you.** Even if it's boring. Especially if it's boring. Find something to count. Notice details. Turn the experience into a game. Your brain will follow.

## Time Flies When You're Doing a Lot

The study found that time perception speeds up with activity density, not emotional valence. You don't need to be happy for time to fly — you just need to be *engaged*.

This is both liberating and dangerous.

Liberating because it means you don't have to wait for fun or joy to make time move. You can manufacture flow through deliberate attention. Take on a hard problem. Learn something new. Engage fully with whatever's in front of you, even if it's difficult or mundane. Your brain will count the handoffs, and time will move.

Dangerous because it means you can lose years to busyness without ever actually *living* them. If your attention is scattered across shallow tasks, notifications, and half-engaged scroll sessions, the count racks up but nothing sticks. You're burning through experiences without metabolizing them. The weeks blur. The months vanish. You look back and wonder where it all went.

This is the trap of modern life: high activity, low attention. Your brain counts it as time passed, but you don't count it as time *lived*.

## What Counts as an Experience?

The ACC doesn't care about clock time. It cares about events. Transitions. Handoffs.

So what counts as an event?

- **A decision.** Choosing one thing over another.
- **A shift in focus.** Moving from one task to another.
- **A moment of noticing.** Seeing something you hadn't seen before.
- **A change in state.** Starting, stopping, pausing, resuming.

The more of these you have, the more time you experience. The fewer, the faster it slips away.

This is why routines can feel both comforting and suffocating. When every day is the same, the count stalls. Your brain stops registering handoffs because there's nothing new to track. Days collapse into an undifferentiated blur.

But when you break the routine — even slightly — the count resumes. A different route to work. A new meal. A conversation with a stranger. These aren't grand adventures, but they're *events*. Your brain counts them, and suddenly the day feels longer, richer, more real.

## Reality Conspires for You When You Invest Your Attention

The UNLV study is a gift to anyone who's ever felt like time was slipping through their fingers.

It tells us that time isn't something that happens *to* us. It's something we construct through attention. The brain doesn't passively record the passage of seconds — it actively counts the experiences we choose to engage with.

This means you have more control than you think.

You can slow time down by bringing deep attention to small moments. You can speed it up by filling your days with engaged activity. You can make memories stick by investing attention fully, or you can let them evaporate by staying half-present.

And here's the pronoia part: **when you invest your attention, reality meets you halfway.** The world becomes more vivid, more textured, more responsive. Not because it changes, but because you're finally *here* to notice it.

Your brain is counting what you care about. It's measuring the depth of your engagement, the quality of your presence, the fidelity of your attention.

So make it count.
